One day, the lowest temperature in Shenyang was -12°C, or 12°C below zero. This is read as “negative 12 degrees Celsius.” This is an example of a negative number. We will see that negative numbers describe many quantities besides temperature. Introducing negative numbers expands the family of numbers and makes it more useful. This chapter introduces negative numbers, extends the number system to rational numbers, and studies their order and operations.

The same day, the high temperature was 3°C above zero and the low was 12°C below zero. These quantities have opposite meanings, so they can be represented by positive and negative numbers. Many quantities in daily life have opposites that can be represented in the same way. For example:
(1) A car travels 3.5 km east and 2.5 km west. If east is defined as positive, west is negative: 3.5 km east is recorded as 3.5 km, and 2.5 km west as -2.5 km.
(2) There is income of 500 yuan and an expense of 237 yuan. First choose one meaning to be positive; the opposite meaning is negative. If income is positive, then an expense is negative: income of 500 yuan is recorded as 500 yuan, and an expense of 237 yuan as -237 yuan.
(3) The water level rises 1.2 m or falls 0.7 m. If a rise is positive, a fall is negative: a rise of 1.2 m is recorded as 1.2 m, and a fall of 0.7 m as -0.7 m.

Summary: Among the numbers above, -12, -2.5, -237, and -0.7 are negative numbers; 3, 3.5, 500, and 1.2 are positive numbers. A plus sign, read as “positive,” may be written before a positive number; for example, 7 may be written as +7. Note that 0 is neither positive nor negative.

The numbers studied so far can be classified as positive integers, such as 1, 2, 3, …; zero, 0; negative integers, such as -1, -2, -3, …; positive fractions, such as 1/3, 22/7, and 4.5 (that is, 4 1/2); and negative fractions, such as -1/2, -2 2/7, and -0.3 (that is, -3/10). Positive and negative integers together with zero are called integers. Positive and negative fractions are called fractions. Integers and fractions together are called rational numbers.

Reading: The word “rational” in “rational number” is related to “ratio.” A rational number can be expressed as the quotient (ratio) of two integers. After studying division of rational numbers in Section 1.10, we will see that this description captures the essence of a rational number.

Numbers can also be grouped into sets. A set of numbers is called a number set. The set of all rational numbers is the set of rational numbers; similarly, we have the set of integers, the set of negative numbers, and the set of nonnegative integers (the natural numbers, including zero), among others.

For some quantities, direction is irrelevant. For example, when calculating how much gasoline a car consumes, we care about the distance traveled, not the direction. Likewise, to find the distance between a point on a number line and the origin, we need only count the unit lengths between them, regardless of which side of the origin the point lies on. The distance from the point representing a number a to the origin is called the absolute value of a, written |a|. For example, the point for +5 is 5 units from the origin, so |+5| = 5; the point for -6 is 6 units from the origin, so |-6| = 6.

Summary: From the meaning of absolute value: (1) the absolute value of a positive number is the number itself; (2) the absolute value of 0 is 0; (3) the absolute value of a negative number is its opposite. Thus the absolute value of every rational number is positive or 0 (that is, nonnegative): |a| >= 0 for every rational number a.

Summary: The addition rules for rational numbers are:
1. To add two numbers with the same sign, keep their common sign and add their absolute values.
2. To add two numbers with different signs and unequal absolute values, use the sign of the addend with the greater absolute value and subtract the smaller absolute value from the greater.
3. The sum of two opposite numbers is 0.
4. Adding 0 to a number leaves that number unchanged.

Section 1.10: Division of Rational Numbers. We learned division in elementary school. What does division mean, and how is it related to multiplication? Try (-6) ÷ 2. If we round it to an integer using the usual rounding rule, we get 3, accurate to the ones place. To one decimal place, it is 3.1; to two decimal places, it is 3.14. In general, if an approximate number is rounded to a specified place, we say it is accurate to that place. For example, a height of 1.70 m is stated to the nearest hundredth of a meter.

Some quantities are difficult to measure exactly, or exact values are unnecessary. We can estimate the required approximate value, sometimes without using ordinary rounding. For example, if a flood affects about 100,000 people and each person needs about 0.5 kg of food per day, about 50,000 kg must be transported daily. Reading: Counting has accompanied human activity since the beginning of civilization. Ancient Chinese records describe “knotting cords” as a way to count. Later, stones and counting rods were used in work and daily life. As mathematical knowledge and technology developed, the abacus and bead arithmetic were invented in China during the Yuan dynasty. In the 17th century, the English invented the slide rule, while the French and Germans developed mechanical calculators for the four arithmetic operations. Today calculators are indispensable in work and everyday life. Although small, they can perform complicated calculations, help solve practical problems, and support understanding of mathematical concepts. Some calculators can run programs, draw graphs, or perform symbolic calculations. Models differ, so their operation may differ as well.

Calculator input generally has two forms. One follows conventional mathematical notation (for example, entering a fraction as a stacked numerator and denominator); another is linear notation (for example, 2/3). Calculator output may be mathematical, linear, or decimal. For 2/3, the decimal output is 0.6666666667. Displays also differ: some calculators show only two rows, while others show several. Many allow formulas to be entered, reviewed, and edited. Take your calculator along as you explore mathematics.

Every fraction is rational, and fractions can be converted to decimals. In long division, every remainder is less than the denominator. Therefore, within at most as many steps as the denominator, division either terminates or repeats. The decimal expansion is either terminating or repeating. For example, -1/3 = -0.333… (often written -0.3 with a dot over the repeating digit), and 22/7 = 3.142857…; both are rational numbers. Conversely, every terminating decimal can be written as a fraction. Can every repeating decimal be written as a fraction? Examine this example and discuss the questions.

0.47 = 0.474747… has repeating block 47. Multiply it by 100 to move the first block to the left of the decimal point: 100 × 0.47 = 47.4747… . The decimal part is still 0.4747…, the original repeating decimal. Thus 100 × 0.47 = 47 + 0.47, so 99 × 0.47 = 47 and 0.47 = 47/99. Does this work for other repeating decimals such as 0.74, 0.8, or 0.325? What about a repeating decimal whose repetition does not begin immediately after the decimal point (a mixed repeating decimal), such as 0.325? Can you transform it into the case above? Chapter summary: This chapter extends the number system to rational numbers and develops their order and operations. It moves from practical experience to mathematical abstraction: negative numbers arise from representing temperatures above and below zero; order comparisons arise from comparing temperatures; and the number line is inspired by a thermometer. Representing rational numbers as points on a number line helps make opposites and absolute values visible and supports understanding order and operations, connecting number and geometry.

When studying rational numbers, consider both sign and absolute value. Many operations and comparisons can be reduced to operations or comparisons of absolute values, which are nonnegative. This reduction to a simpler familiar case is a common mathematical idea. The defining property of a rational number is that it can be written as a quotient of integers. Recognizing this is important for understanding rational numbers and for later extensions of the number system. Some numbers encountered later cannot be expressed as a ratio of two integers, so the number system must be extended further.

Extending the number system changes what is possible. Before negative numbers were introduced, subtracting a larger number from a smaller one was not possible within the nonnegative numbers. Among rational numbers, any two numbers can be subtracted, and subtraction can be rewritten as addition. Zero is also a specific number, not merely “nothing”: on the number line it is the origin and the boundary between positive and negative numbers. The expanded system retains important properties of the original one, especially the laws of arithmetic. These shared properties will remain important in later mathematics.

This chapter rebuilds and extends the nonnegative-number ideas learned in elementary school. Analogy and reduction to simpler cases are important methods. The textbook emphasizes reasoning while exploring and summarizing new ideas, and the exercises provide further practice. Letters are introduced both to prepare for algebraic expressions in the next chapter and to express general rules, laying the groundwork for more advanced reasoning and proof.
